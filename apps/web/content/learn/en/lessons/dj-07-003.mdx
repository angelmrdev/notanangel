Two tracks can be perfectly beatmatched and still sound muddy when their kick drums and basslines play together. A **bass swap** is a deliberate handover of low-frequency dominance from the outgoing deck to the incoming deck. Its purpose is not to make the music louder: it is to make the transition *clearer*.

In the previous lessons, you learned to use EQ musically and set sensible channel levels. Here you combine those skills with phrase alignment from the beatmatching module.

## Learning goals

1. Recognise when overlapping kicks and basslines compete for space.
2. Prepare the incoming track with a reduced LOW band, without confusing the EQ knob with channel gain.
3. Decide where a bass handover belongs in an eight- or sixteen-bar phrase.
4. Compare a direct swap with a progressive exchange.
5. Rehearse the move and diagnose unwanted bass build-up, a missing kick and poor timing.

## 1. Why bass needs special attention

Kick drums and basslines occupy much of the low-frequency energy that gives dance music its impact. When both tracks contribute a strong low end simultaneously, the blend can feel heavy, unclear or louder than expected. The exact result depends on the songs, their phase relationship, arrangement and the EQ response; **two basslines do not always produce a predictable volume increase**.

You are listening for *which track is carrying the groove*. In some mixes both low ends can coexist briefly; in others, one dominant bass foundation gives a much cleaner handover.

| Sound you notice | Possible interpretation | Practical response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two kicks appear to land together but sound oversized | Low-frequency overlap is too dense | Reduce the incoming LOW before opening B; plan a handover |
| Kick feels thin during the drop | Both tracks may be cut in the lows | Restore the incoming LOW at the intended downbeat |
| Bass sounds clean but the vocals clash | The problem is in other frequency bands or phrasing | Fix the arrangement or mids; do not keep turning LOW |
| The output meter jumps when B enters | Combined signal may exceed available headroom | Recheck channel balance, EQ and master metering |

**LOW is not a universal bass on/off switch.** On one mixer the bottom of the knob can be a full kill; on another it may be a finite attenuation. EQ frequency boundaries also differ, and cutting LOW may affect part of the kick while leaving other bass harmonics audible.

## 2. Plan the handover on a musical boundary

A beginner-friendly structure is to let the outgoing deck **A** carry the bass at first, blend the incoming deck **B** with its LOW reduced, then hand over low-frequency dominance on a new musical section. This is often a downbeat at the start of a phrase.

Try an **8-bar overlap**, change on **bar 5**, then compare a direct handover and a gradual exchange. Next choose **16 bars** with the handover at bar 9. These are practice templates, not fixed rules. A track may have a breakdown, no kick at the start, a bass fill, or a different musical cue that calls for another choice.

## 3. Perform a simple bass swap

1. Choose two tempo-compatible tracks with a recognisable phrase change.
2. Check their loud sections and set conservative channel gains; do not boost LOW simply to match loudness.
3. Prelisten to B and choose a launch point that aligns its phrase with A.
4. While A plays, prepare B with its LOW reduced and the channel level controlled.
5. Introduce B over eight or sixteen bars. Listen for midrange conflicts as well as rhythm.
6. On the planned **downbeat**, reduce A's low-frequency contribution as you restore the low end of B. Depending on the mixer and songs, this can be a quick coordinated exchange or a shorter staged move.
7. Let B become the main track; complete the exit of A without adding an unintended level jump.
8. Listen back to a recording. Ask whether the new kick takes over confidently and whether the overall level stays controlled.

**What about a sudden cut?** A sharp swap can create impact when the incoming track has a strong kick on the first beat. A gradual handover can sound smoother if both songs have sustained low-frequency elements. Neither is automatically better; count the phrase and trust your ears.

## 4. Troubleshooting a bass handover

<BassSwapDiagnosisEn />

Two important distinctions:

- **EQ is not a fix for beatmatching.** If kicks are drifting apart, correct the tempo or alignment first.
- **EQ is not a fix for clipping upstream.** A channel can already be distorted before the EQ or master, depending on the mixer signal path. Inspect the actual channel and output meters.

Do not assume turning both LOW knobs to a specific clock position creates a neutral result. Work from the sound of the two tracks and the behaviour of your mixer.

## 5. Practice: rehearse two versions

Use an 8-bar overlap first. Record one **direct** swap on a downbeat, then repeat with a **progressive** exchange. Compare clarity, perceived energy and the level through the transition. If they sound different, describe *why* rather than simply declaring one better.

### Self-assessment

1. **Why reduce B's LOW before its entry?** To avoid having two strong low-frequency foundations competing immediately.
2. **When is a useful place to restore B's LOW?** Often a meaningful downbeat or phrase change, if the arrangement supports it.
3. **Does an EQ knob marked LOW affect the same frequencies on every DJ mixer?** No.
4. **If both kicks are drifting, should you fix it by swapping LOW?** No; check beatmatching first.
5. **What should you watch as well as the EQ?** The channel and master levels, phrasing and the sound of the overlap.
