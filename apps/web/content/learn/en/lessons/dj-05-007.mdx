Hot Cues, Memory Cues and saved loops help you **find, trigger and repeat useful musical moments** during DJ preparation and live mixing. They are not a substitute for listening, counting phrases or checking the beatgrid. This lesson turns those tools into a repeatable workflow you can use on a controller or in DJ software.

**By the end:** you will distinguish cue types, design a sensible color code, set functional cue points, create musical 4/8/16-beat loops and verify your work after export.

## 1. What is a cue in DJ software?

The word **cue** is used for several different actions. A transport CUE button commonly returns to a temporary cue position (the exact behavior varies by deck and settings); a **Memory Cue** is typically a stored location for navigation; and a **Hot Cue** is a saved point you can trigger directly from a pad, keyboard or controller. A **saved loop** stores a range between IN and OUT, not only one position. Knowing the difference prevents accidental jumps during a live set.

A temporary cue is useful when rehearsing an entry. A memory marker may help you identify the start of a breakdown without triggering it. A Hot Cue can jump instantly to a vocal or drop, potentially interrupting whatever is playing. You should never assume they are interchangeable across software brands or hardware.

## 2. Choose useful musical locations

Mark sections you can explain without looking at a timer: an intro with a clean rhythmic entry, the first vocal, a breakdown, a chorus and a mix-friendly outro. Not every track needs all five. A short pop edit might only require an intro, vocal and exit marker; a longer club track may justify additional markers near builds and instrumental phrases.

First listen and count phrases. A cue at the first loud transient is not necessarily a cue at **beat one**. If the song starts with a pickup, a speech sample or an off-beat accent, choose a musically useful downbeat instead. Label the location or use consistent colors, then test the entry with headphones at a safe level.

## 3. Build a color convention that makes sense

Colors are most valuable when they tell you what a pad does **before** you press it. For example, blue can mean intro, pink first vocal, yellow breakdown and green outro. This is merely a proposed scheme; the right one is the scheme you remember and that your software, exporter and playback hardware can show consistently.

Avoid changing color meanings from track to track. If you DJ different genres, keep the core meanings stable and add descriptive labels only when needed. Some hardware uses fewer colors, pads or cue banks than your software. Check the actual export rather than assuming that everything travels with the file.

## 4. Prepare cues in a reliable order

**Check your beatgrid before relying on Quantize.** In lesson DJ-05-006 we learned to distinguish a shifted grid from a drifting one. An incorrect downbeat may place a quantized Hot Cue off the musical pulse even though the pad appears to be in the right section.

Use a repeatable sequence: listen to the full arrangement, verify BPM and grid, choose a small number of musical reference locations, save and color the points, then trigger every marker while monitoring privately. Repeat the test after closing and reopening the application. If a cue causes an undesirable jump or loud entry, move it or remove it rather than keeping it for appearance.

## 5. Understand 4-, 8- and 16-beat loops

A **loop** repeats the audio between its IN and OUT boundaries. On a typical 4/4 dance track, a 4-beat loop spans one bar, 8 beats spans two bars and 16 beats spans four bars. Some applications show counts of beats while others show bars or fractions; check the notation before practicing. Never confuse an eight-beat loop with eight whole bars.

Loop length alone does not guarantee a musical result. A phrase may contain a fill, vocal pickup, crash or reverb tail that sounds awkward at the repeat boundary. Start by testing an instrumental passage with stable rhythm, then compare 4, 8 and 16 beats. If a shorter loop cuts off a vocal or repeatedly lands mid-phrase, pick another region.

## 6. Quantize, timing and the corrected grid

**Quantize** can align a cue trigger or loop boundary with a rhythmic division set by the software. It is not a substitute for choosing the right musical point. Different DJ programs have different quantize settings, and some controls only quantize certain actions. With Quantize off, your timing matters more directly; with it on, the action may snap or be delayed to the next permitted division.

Practice with an audible metronomic passage: trigger a cue slightly early, exactly on the beat and slightly late, comparing results with the feature enabled and disabled. Test the same passage with an 8-beat loop. If a loop consistently lands off-beat, revisit the grid and the IN/OUT positions before blaming the controller.

## 7. Save, export and test on the real equipment

A cue that works on the laptop is not automatically available on every USB player, controller or application. Hot Cue counts, color support, saved loops, memory markers and bank mappings vary across ecosystems. Treat **collection metadata**, the underlying audio file and the performance export as separate layers.

After preparing a small selection, close the app and open it again; verify the markers. Export to your intended media where supported and test with the exact player or controller you plan to use. Keep a recoverable library backup before mass changes. When using an external SSD, avoid moving files outside the program without a relocation plan.

## 8. Practice: prepare three tracks

Choose three files you can legally access and use in your DJ software: a stable 4/4 track, a short radio edit and a song with an unusual intro. For each, listen to the structure, check the grid, set a small number of functional Hot Cues and try a **4-beat, 8-beat and 16-beat** loop in a suitable section. Write down which version sounds musical and why.

Finally reopen the application and confirm the points are still there. If your equipment supports export, test one track on its target device. A successful exercise is not “eight colored pads”; it is being able to explain where every marker leads and make a reliable musical entry without looking away from the mixer.

## Troubleshooting quick reference

| Symptom | Likely cause | First action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hot Cue starts off-beat | Misaligned grid or Quantize setting | Verify beatgrid and trigger timing |
| Saved loop clicks or feels awkward | Boundary cuts a phrase or sound | Change IN/OUT or choose another phrase |
| Cue disappears on a different player | Unsupported export or cue type | Test device support and export path |
| Pad triggers an unexpected section | Wrong bank or deck selected | Check deck focus, pad mode and label |
