**Beatmatching by ear** means listening to two tracks and making small timing and speed adjustments until their beats stay together. The goal is not to make both BPM numbers look identical: it is to make two pulses coincide *and remain together* for long enough to mix reliably. You can learn the core technique with two decks, headphones and a pair of simple, steady tracks.

This lesson builds on [tempo, pitch and playback speed](/learn-to-dj/beatmatching/tempo-pitch-playback-speed). In that lesson, we separated the sustained **tempo fader** from a temporary **jog nudge / pitch bend**. Here you will use both, guided by what you hear. Exact hardware behaviours vary; test your own equipment privately.

## Learning objectives

By the end, you should be able to:

1. Find and count a steady four-beat pulse without watching a waveform.
2. Start an incoming track on a clear reference beat while monitoring it in headphones.
3. Hear whether the incoming deck is ahead of or behind the reference.
4. Distinguish a fixed beat offset from drift caused by different average tempos.
5. Use a short nudge for position and the tempo fader for lasting speed, then verify by listening.

## 1. Make one track your reference

Call the playing track **Deck A**. Keep it at a steady tempo and audible through MASTER at a comfortable level. Deck B is the **incoming track**; initially keep its channel fader closed so your practice does not reach the room output. Select B's headphone CUE. If your mixer provides a cue/master blend, use it to hear A and B together in the headphones. Otherwise, use an appropriate monitoring technique supported by your equipment.

Choose two tracks with clear kick drums and simple, regular sections, ideally close in BPM: for example 124 and 126 BPM. Avoid variable-tempo live recordings, long rubato intros and busy percussion while learning. Make sure the tracks and headphones are routed correctly before touching the jog.

**Count A aloud:** “one, two, three, four” in time with the kick or other reliable pulse. The musical *downbeat* is commonly beat 1 of the bar, but do not confuse starting on a strong beat with selecting the correct phrase. Phrase alignment will become especially important when you build full transitions.

## 2. Launch B, then listen before reacting

Prepare B at a clear beat. Press Play on B in time with an A beat, keeping B in CUE. If you miss the start badly, stop and try again. A clean launch is easier to interpret than a rushed chain of random corrections.

What you may hear:

- **One tight, unified thump:** the kick positions are close enough for the moment. Continue listening.
- **A double thump or flam:** one kick lands a little before the other. The tracks have a timing offset.
- **A changing double thump:** they may be moving apart because their average tempos differ.

It can be hard to tell which sound belongs to which track. Briefly monitor B alone, then compare against A, or use distinct percussion timbres. Avoid basing the whole decision on a single kick; listen over several beats.

## 3. Hear which deck is early or late

**Ahead / early:** B's beat arrives before the corresponding beat in A. Briefly **slow B down** with an appropriate jog action or pitch bend so its next beat lands later.

**Behind / late:** B's beat arrives after A's beat. Briefly **speed B up** to move its next beat earlier.

Always make a small correction, release the control and listen again. A jog wheel's effect depends on jog mode, vinyl mode, deck state and controller mapping. Some hardware scratches or seeks instead of nudging. Use the setting intended for temporary pitch bending, not a scratch movement that interrupts the track.

A shortcut is to imagine A as a train you must stay beside. If B runs ahead of the train, briefly brake; if B falls behind, briefly catch up. **Do not** permanently change the fader just to move an otherwise correctly paced beat a few milliseconds.

## 4. Fixed offset vs. tempo drift

These are different faults and need different solutions:

| What you hear | What it usually means | First response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Double kicks remain the same distance apart | Same or very similar tempo, different beat position | Brief jog nudge |
| Kicks align, then gradually separate again | Average tempo is different | Small sustained tempo adjustment, then nudge |
| Kicks never settle in a predictable pattern | Wrong counted pulse, syncopated drums or variable timing | Verify the reference beat and choose simpler material |
| Kicks seem aligned but phrases sound awkward | Beat alignment is not musical phrase alignment | Check bar and phrase starts before attempting a transition |

A deck running at 126 BPM against 124 BPM is about **2 beats per minute faster**. Even if you start exactly together, the faster deck eventually moves ahead. Nudging it repeatedly may hide the issue for a moment, but only a sustained tempo correction fixes that average-speed mismatch.

## 5. The adjustment loop: launch → listen → nudge → hold

This is the repeatable ear-training loop:

1. **Launch** B near a clear beat of A.
2. **Listen** to both kicks and decide whether B is early, late or apparently aligned.
3. **Nudge** B briefly to remove any fixed timing gap.
4. **Hold** for 16–32 beats with your hands off the jog.
5. If the gap **grows**, make a *small lasting adjustment* to B's tempo fader in the right direction: if B keeps moving ahead, slow its sustained tempo; if it falls behind, increase it. Nudge again to align and repeat.
6. Once alignment holds, count bars and check that the next musical phrase also makes sense. Do not raise B's channel fader yet unless you are deliberately practising a controlled transition.

**Important:** small fader increments are easier to judge than dramatic changes. You are not expected to solve the whole problem with a single move. Keep Sync turned off for this specific practice, and do not trust the analysed BPM value without listening.

## 6. Guided two-deck practice

Work for roughly 10–15 minutes on just one pair of tracks. You can use local files or another supported source that works with your setup. The exercise does not require publishing or recording the songs.

<EarPracticeEn />

**Round A — equal BPM:** choose two stable tracks that already match. Practise starting B and nudging away a fixed gap without touching the tempo fader. Your target is 32 beats of stable alignment.

**Round B — close BPM:** choose tracks about 1–3 BPM apart. Correct B's sustained tempo, align it and listen for another 32 beats. Repeat until you can predict the direction of the fader change by ear.

**Round C — screen-free:** cover or ignore the BPM and stacked waveforms. Make a diagnosis, execute a correction, and only then look at the displays to check your prediction. The displays are a verification aid, not the answer key: analysis and beatgrids can be wrong.

If monitoring A and B together is difficult, first practise tapping and counting over A alone, then introduce the second signal. Lower the headphone volume if your hearing feels fatigued.

## 7. Common beginner mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | Better approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moving the tempo fader for every small double kick | Changes sustained speed when only timing is wrong | Nudge first; evaluate for drift |
| Nudging continuously for a whole phrase | Masks a BPM mismatch | Let go and listen for a growing gap |
| Watching two BPM labels rather than listening | BPM analysis can be inaccurate | Count and compare the actual pulse |
| Scratching when intending to bend | Jog is in an unsuitable mode | Check jog / vinyl behaviour in your software |
| Mixing two very busy or irregular songs initially | Makes kick identification difficult | Start with simple percussion |
| Bringing B to MASTER before verifying routing | Can create an unintended audible clash | Keep B channel down during the lab |
| Matching kicks but ignoring the musical downbeat | Beats can match in the wrong bar or phrase | Count bars before a real transition |

## 8. Self-check

Try to answer before reading the solutions:

1. B's kick always arrives a little *after* A's kick, but the distance stays constant. What is your first move?
2. B starts aligned, then gradually gets *ahead* of A. Should you nudge forever or adjust sustained tempo?
3. Do two displays showing 128 BPM prove that the beats are aligned?
4. Why should you listen for 16–32 beats after a correction?

**Answers:** (1) Briefly speed up / nudge B forward, then release. (2) Reduce B's sustained tempo a little, realign with a nudge and test again. (3) No; BPM is speed, not beat position, and analysis may be wrong. (4) A longer listening window reveals ongoing tempo drift that a one-beat check cannot.

## Key takeaway and next lesson

**Your ears diagnose; your controls respond.** A constant timing gap calls for a temporary position correction. A steadily growing gap calls for a lasting tempo correction followed by realignment. Once you can recognise both patterns, the next lesson explores **BPM Sync versus Beat Sync**, what each automates and what you still need to verify yourself.
