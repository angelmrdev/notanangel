An EQ is not just a tone control. When two tracks overlap, it can help each track occupy a clearer role. The goal is **not** to turn every knob or to make everything louder: it is to preserve musical detail and a consistent listening level.

In the equipment module you met the mixer controls. Here you will use them **musically**, in the context of a real two-deck transition. You do not need an expensive mixer: a standard three-band EQ is enough to practise.

## By the end of this lesson

1. Identify common sound sources in low, mid and high bands.
2. Explain why EQ bands overlap and why their exact ranges differ between mixers.
3. Distinguish **cutting space** from **boosting level**.
4. Use a two-deck EQ experiment to prepare a simple transition.
5. Reset the controls, listen objectively and keep volume safe.

## 1. What do LOW, MID and HIGH actually change?

Most beginner DJ mixers provide three bands per channel. They are **broad tonal regions**, not three perfectly separated containers. Typical sources include:

| Band | What to listen for | A common mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **LOW** | Kick weight, bass line and low synth fundamentals | Leaving both bass-heavy tracks fully open during a busy overlap |
| **MID** | Vocals, chords, snare body and the main melodic hook | Cutting so deeply that the character of a track disappears |
| **HIGH** | Hi-hats, cymbals, crisp consonants and brightness | Boosting highs until the blend becomes sharp or tiring |

A kick drum has energy beyond the low band; vocals span mids and highs; synths can cover the entire spectrum. The crossover points, steepness, gain ranges and **isolator vs ordinary EQ** behaviour depend on your hardware. Do not assign a universal frequency number to each knob.

### Develop your listening vocabulary

Listen to an instrumental segment and turn **one EQ band at a time**, gently, then return it to its neutral position. Describe the *specific sound* that becomes less prominent. This is more useful than simply saying “the mix sounds worse.”

## 2. Cutting is different from boosting

A **cut** reduces a region; a **boost** increases it. Both can change perceived loudness. On many mixers the centre detent means **0 dB EQ adjustment**, not silence, and turning the EQ fully left does not necessarily remove that band completely unless the mixer uses an isolator-style design.

**EQ does not replace input gain or channel faders.** A large boost may eat into headroom, and turning down a band cannot repair distortion that already occurred upstream. Check the gain stage and the appropriate channel/master meters separately.

For beginner transitions, a modest, purposeful cut often creates space more predictably than boosting the incoming channel. Always decide by listening, not by treating preset knob positions as rules.

## 3. Two-deck example: make room for the incoming bass

Imagine deck A is already playing a four-on-the-floor track with a clear bass line. Deck B is cued and beatmatched. Both tracks have strong low-end elements.

1. With B still in headphones, set its EQ to neutral and identify its kick and bass.
2. Reduce B's LOW before introducing it, leaving A to carry the low end.
3. Bring B into the mix gradually at a suitable phrase boundary. Listen to the mids and highs as well as the rhythm.
4. When the arrangement allows, **exchange the low-end emphasis**: gradually reduce A's LOW and restore B's LOW.
5. Complete the transition, then return the remaining channel to a sensible neutral EQ setup for the next track.

This is **one useful technique, not an obligatory transition formula**. If B has no bass, A's outro is already sparse, or the arrangement changes unexpectedly, a different move may sound better. Avoid abrupt bass drops unless they are intentional.

The interactive diagnosis above is a *conceptual cue*, not a signal-processing engine. Two bass signals do not simply add by summing their EQ knob values. Their actual interaction depends on audio content, timing, phases and the playback system.

## 4. Mistakes to recognise early

| You notice… | Possible reason | What to try |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The mix becomes muddy | Competing kick/bass or dense arrangements | Give one track a clearer low-end role; shorten the overlap |
| The incoming track sounds thin | Too much LOW cut, or missing midrange body | Restore some LOW, reassess MID and listen in context |
| Vocals fight each other | Both tracks carry strong lead vocals | Change phrase timing or choose a more instrumental section; EQ alone may not solve it |
| The transition sounds dramatically louder | Boosts, gain mismatch or combined energy | Recheck gain staging, EQ boosts and channel levels |
| The sound is harsh | Excess HIGH boost or busy high-frequency content | Return HIGH toward neutral and reassess |

**Watch your hearing:** test at modest monitor/headphone volume. Do not push a master into clipping to “hear the EQ better.”

## 5. Guided practice: listen, cut, reset, compare

You need two authorised practice tracks with similar tempos, headphones and a mixer or controller with channel EQ. Keep Sync and beatmatching settings as you prefer: the objective here is to train your *EQ decisions*, not to relearn deck synchronisation.

### Quick self-check

1. Does turning LOW down remove *every* component of the kick? **No.** A kick spans multiple frequency regions.
2. Is the 12 o'clock EQ position always the exact same filter response? **No.** Device designs differ; it is commonly the neutral gain position.
3. If two vocals clash, is cutting MID always enough? **No.** Better phrase selection may be needed.
4. Should you boost both LOW knobs to make a transition stronger? **Not by default.** It can increase masking and reduce headroom.
5. What should you do after experimenting? **Reset, compare at matched listening levels and evaluate by ear.**
