**Sync is an assistant, not a substitute for listening.** When two tracks play together, you need their *average speed* to agree and their *individual beats* to coincide. Even if both conditions are satisfied, their bars and phrases may still be placed badly. Different DJ applications and controllers give their Sync modes different names and behaviours; always consult your software's current manual.

This lesson follows [beatmatching by ear](/learn-to-dj/beatmatching/how-to-beatmatch-by-ear), where you learned to distinguish a constant offset from a drifting beat. Here you will compare automatic assistance with the manual decisions you already know.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain the difference between **tempo matching** and **beat/phase alignment**.
2. Describe typical **BPM Sync** and **Beat Sync** behaviour without assuming every platform works identically.
3. Recognize wrong beatgrids, unsuitable downbeats and variable-tempo material.
4. Switch between assisted and manual control while checking the physical tempo fader.
5. Choose whether Sync helps a transition, then verify the result by ear.

## 1. Two independent problems: speed and position

Imagine Deck A running at **128 BPM** and Deck B at **124 BPM**. Even if you start both kicks together, B will fall behind: its beats are spaced farther apart. This is a **tempo difference**. Making both tracks play at 128 BPM solves the average-speed mismatch, but B may still land *between* A's kicks if you launched it late. That is a **phase or beat-position offset**.

| Control or condition | What it changes | What it does not guarantee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tempo fader | Sustained playback speed | The next kick lands together |
| Brief jog nudge / pitch bend | Temporary beat position | Long-term equal tempo |
| Typical BPM Sync | Tempo of an incoming deck relative to a reference | Correct kick position or musical phrase |
| Typical Beat Sync | Tempo and beat position using analysed beatgrid information | A correct beatgrid, downbeat, phrase or great-sounding mix |

The labels are not universal: some systems have one Sync button with configurable modes, and some respond differently to a jog nudge while Sync is active.

## 2. What BPM Sync usually does

**BPM Sync** normally makes the incoming track's tempo follow a designated reference or master deck. If the tracks start at 124 and 128 BPM, the incoming playback rate adjusts toward the reference. However, two sequences of equally spaced kicks can still be separated by half a beat. BPM equality is not proof of phase alignment.

A practical workflow is: identify which deck is master, engage BPM Sync on the intended incoming deck, listen in CUE, then correct the remaining beat-position offset if necessary. Do not confuse the master deck with the MASTER audio output; the reference/tempo master and the audible main mix are separate concepts.

## 3. What Beat Sync usually adds

**Beat Sync** typically uses a track's analysed **beatgrid** to match both playback tempo and the position of corresponding beats. When the grid is accurate, it can reduce the need for a manual jog nudge. But a grid is an estimate of musical timing, not an infallible record of what the drummer actually played.

Beat Sync does **not** know whether your desired mix should begin on bar 1 of an eight-bar intro, after a breakdown or on the start of a 16-bar phrase. Two perfectly synchronized kicks can sound awkward if vocal phrases or harmonic changes clash. Correcting phrase placement is a separate musical skill.

## 4. Why the display can mislead you

Check these four common scenarios before blaming your ears:

- **Beatgrid placed incorrectly:** grid lines are consistently offset from actual kicks; Beat Sync may align the *grid* instead of what you hear.
- **Wrong counted beat or downbeat:** kicks coincide but the musical phrases are displaced; start at the appropriate beat 1 of the chosen bar or phrase.
- **Variable tempo or live drums:** the tempo may change over time; a single fixed grid may not describe the whole section.
- **Wrong sync reference:** the incoming track may be following the deck you did not intend. Verify which deck is master and who follows it.

## 5. How to turn Sync off without losing control

Turning Sync off is not guaranteed to restore a deck to its **original track BPM**. Depending on the application, the current playback tempo can remain at the synchronized value until you move the physical tempo fader. Some controllers use **soft takeover / pickup**, where the physical fader must reach the software's current value before controlling it again. Others use different takeover rules.

Before you disengage Sync during a live mix, check the displayed BPM, understand how your unit handles its fader and monitor the audio. Avoid a large fader movement while the channel is audible. For learning, keep Deck B out of the room mix and test this behaviour slowly in headphones. **Never assume switching Sync off instantly returns B to its original BPM.**

## 6. A controlled A/B practice lab

Use two tracks with steady drum sections, ideally around 124 and 128 BPM. Start with Deck A on MASTER and Deck B in headphone CUE, its channel fader closed. First try manual timing, then BPM Sync, then Beat Sync. Finish by disabling Sync and reproducing the correction without automation.

<SyncPracticeEn />

**Round A — speed without position:** engage BPM Sync, intentionally launch B a little late and notice that the BPM values can match while the kicks remain separate. Nudge B until the beats coincide.

**Round B — automatic position:** try Beat Sync with a verified grid, then listen for a complete phrase. Check if the transitions occur on the intended bar and whether the next section feels musically right.

**Round C — return to manual:** disengage Sync with B still in headphones. Watch for physical-fader pickup, confirm the actual speed and make small corrections. Repeat the same transition with Sync entirely off.

## 7. Common mistakes and corrections

| Mistake | Why it fails | Better approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assuming equal BPM means aligned kicks | Speed and phase are separate | Listen and nudge if required |
| Trusting Beat Sync on an inaccurate grid | Grid lines may not represent real kicks | Verify and correct the beatgrid |
| Ignoring bar and phrase boundaries | Beat alignment is not arrangement alignment | Count bars and choose entry points |
| Turning Sync off and abruptly moving the fader | Soft takeover may change how the fader responds | Inspect BPM, test pickup and adjust gradually |
| Treating Sync as cheating | Automation and mixing judgement serve different functions | Learn manual skills and use automation intentionally |

## Self-check

1. If both decks display 128 BPM but a double kick is audible, what is probably missing?
2. Which typical Sync mode also attempts to align beat positions?
3. Why can Beat Sync align kick drums but still produce a bad transition?
4. Does switching Sync off always return playback to the track's original BPM?
5. What should you inspect before moving a physical tempo fader after disengaging Sync?

**Answers:** (1) Phase/beat-position alignment, possibly needing a jog nudge. (2) Beat Sync. (3) Incorrect phrase/downbeat placement or other musical clashes. (4) No; behaviour varies and synchronized speed can remain. (5) The displayed actual BPM, fader position and pickup/takeover behaviour.

## Key takeaway

**BPM Sync helps with sustained speed. Beat Sync usually also helps with phase, if the grid is correct. Neither guarantees a musically good transition.** Count your bars, listen to the actual kicks and know how to regain manual control. In the next lesson, you will apply these principles to beatgrids and their corrections.
