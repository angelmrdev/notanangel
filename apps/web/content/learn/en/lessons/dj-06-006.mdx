**A mix can start perfectly aligned and still fall apart after a few bars.** That is *drift*: a growing separation between the pulses of two tracks. Unlike a late start, drift usually means their playback rates do not match, or one recording does not have a steady tempo. This lesson teaches the repeatable cycle **listen → diagnose → adjust tempo → correct position → listen again**.

You already learnt to prepare Deck B privately in [How to Cue a Track in DJ Headphones](/learn-to-dj/beatmatching/how-to-cue-dj-tracks). Now keep B in CUE long enough to judge whether your correction actually lasts.

## Learning objectives

By the end you can:

1. Hear the difference between **fixed offset** and **progressive drift**.
2. Work out which deck is running faster or slower over several bars.
3. Use the **tempo fader** for a growing difference and a **small jog nudge** for the remaining position error.
4. Test whether a correction holds for 16–32 bars rather than just one kick.
5. Recognise when a variable-tempo recording makes a single fixed BPM insufficient.

## 1. One late kick is not the same as drift

When A and B are initially separated by a short but **stable** interval, they may already share the same BPM: B simply started late or early. A brief nudge adjusts their *relative position*. If the separation grows while both tracks keep playing, nudge alone will not solve it: the *rate* needs correction.

| What you hear | Likely issue | First corrective action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A double kick stays equally far apart | Fixed beat offset | Gently nudge B into position |
| Kicks start aligned, then diverge | BPM mismatch | Fine-tune B's tempo, then nudge |
| Kicks match but musical bar 1 is misplaced | Incorrect bar entry | Re-cue and relaunch at the correct downbeat |
| Kicks gradually diverge despite matched displayed BPM | Inaccurate analysis or variable tempo | Trust your ears; verify and adjust |

This follows the same diagnostic language introduced in [How to Beatmatch by Ear](/learn-to-dj/beatmatching/how-to-beatmatch-by-ear), but here the priority is **stability across time**, not the first alignment.

## 2. How a tiny BPM mismatch adds up

If both decks start on the same beat and play at fixed rates, the approximate accumulated number of beats separating them after `t` seconds is:

**Accumulated beat difference = (B BPM − A BPM) × t ÷ 60.**

For example, A at 128 BPM and B at 127.5 BPM gain a difference of **−0.25 beat in 30 seconds**: B falls behind. At A's tempo that is approximately **117 ms**. The signs indicate which deck gets ahead; this is a simplified constant-tempo calculation, not an audio measurement.

The point is not to calculate milliseconds in a club. It is to recognise why a half-BPM error matters in a long blend. A jog nudge may make the decks sound tight *now*, but the beat gap will build again if their BPM remain different.

## 3. Identify which way the tempo needs to move

Start with two tracks near the same BPM and a clearly audible kick. With A playing to the room, listen to B in headphones. Align B once, then **leave your hands off the controls for 8–16 bars**.

- **B moves progressively ahead:** B is effectively faster. Lower B's tempo slightly.
- **B drops progressively behind:** B is effectively slower. Raise B's tempo slightly.
- **The gap never grows:** their average rates may already match; fix the position instead.
- **The error changes direction irregularly:** check for live drums, tempo changes, an inaccurate beatgrid or a difficult rhythmic pattern.

If the direction is unclear from headphones, briefly isolate kick-heavy sections or compare the transient pairs by ear. Waveforms can help confirm an observation but are not proof of what the music is doing.

## 4. A practical correction sequence

1. **Start together:** choose matching downbeats and launch B while its channel is closed to the master.
2. **Observe:** listen for 8–16 bars. Is the offset fixed or increasing?
3. **Correct rate:** if the gap grows, move B's tempo fader a very small amount in the needed direction.
4. **Correct position:** nudge B with the jog or pitch bend to close the remaining gap. Do not use a long nudge to disguise a persistent BPM mismatch.
5. **Verify:** keep listening for another 16–32 bars without touching the decks. If drift returns, repeat in smaller adjustments.
6. **Bring B in only when stable:** double-check the phrase entrance and channel levels before opening the blend.

If you are learning with an adjustable pitch range, use a narrower range where appropriate for finer fader resolution. Do not make aggressive jumps while B is audible to the audience.

## 5. Why a perfect beatgrid can still mislead you

The displayed BPM may be rounded; the analysis may be wrong; a track may accelerate subtly or drift around a nominal tempo. A quantised or beat-synced visual grid cannot change the original musical phrasing or guarantee that live percussion stays perfectly steady. For **genuinely variable-tempo** material, you may need ongoing manual corrections or a transition that does not depend on a prolonged beat-for-beat blend.

Do not confuse drift with a bar/phrase mismatch. Two perfectly matched kick pulses can still produce an awkward musical transition when the sections are misaligned. Revisit [How to Align Beats, Bars and Phrases](/learn-to-dj/beatmatching/align-beats-and-phrases) if the tempo is stable but the result does not make musical sense.

## 6. Practice with two decks

Use two 4/4 tracks with clear, steady kicks. To make the difference audible, deliberately place B at **127.5 BPM while A is at 128 BPM**, then reset both and repeat with no waveform watching. Keep B out of the main mix until the correction holds.

<DriftPracticeEn />

Try three rounds: **(a)** a fixed late launch at identical BPM, **(b)** a small tempo mismatch, **(c)** a track with a less regular groove. Record what you heard, which control you used and how many bars the result remained stable. If the recording itself varies in tempo, do not force it into a constant-BPM result.

## 7. Mistakes to avoid

- **Nudging every four beats forever:** solve the underlying rate mismatch instead.
- **Moving the fader to fix a one-off launch:** tempo affects what happens next, not just where the beat is now.
- **Trusting identical displayed BPM:** rounding, analysis and variable tempo can fool you.
- **Correcting too aggressively:** small changes are easier to judge and sound smoother.
- **Ignoring the bar or phrase:** an in-time mix can be musically mistimed.
- **Opening B too early:** confirm a stable pre-listen before fading it into master.

## Self-check

1. If the kick offset is unchanged for 16 bars, which control should you consider first?
2. If B falls increasingly behind A, should you speed up B or slow it down?
3. At 128 BPM versus 127.5 BPM, how many beats of separation accumulate in 30 seconds if both started aligned?
4. Why can a jog adjustment alone fail to fix progressive drift?
5. What changes when a track contains genuine tempo variation?

**Answers:** (1) Jog/pitch bend to fix position. (2) Speed B up slightly. (3) 0.25 beat; B falls behind. (4) It fixes position but not the ongoing rate difference. (5) A single constant BPM may not hold the alignment; keep monitoring and adapt your approach.

## Key takeaway

**First correct the speed, then the position, then test again.** What matters is not whether the kicks line up for a moment, but whether they remain together through the section you intend to mix.
