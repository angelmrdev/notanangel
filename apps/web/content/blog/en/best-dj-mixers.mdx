**A standalone DJ mixer is not automatically the next upgrade after a beginner controller.** It serves a different purpose: combining audio from external sources while giving you level controls, EQ, cue monitoring and, on some models, filters and effects. But unlike most entry-level controllers, a standalone mixer **doesn't necessarily give you play buttons, jog wheels or a way to browse tracks on your laptop**.

If you don't already own decks, turntables or suitable audio sources, buying a mixer can commit you to a much larger setup than the price tag suggests. This guide compares three very different mixers already listed in the Not an Angel product catalogue: the **Allen & Heath Xone:23**, **Allen & Heath Xone:96** and **Pioneer DJ DJM-A9**.

**Short answer:** choose the **Xone:23** for a relatively straightforward external two-channel mixing setup; investigate the **Xone:96** when you need analogue filters, flexible routing and dual USB audio interfaces; consider the **DJM-A9** for a professional four-channel club workflow with built-in effects. None is necessarily the best first purchase if you only have a laptop.

## 1. Allen & Heath Xone:23: direct external-source mixing

<ProductCard productId="allen-heath-xone23" />

The **Xone:23** is about working directly with the audio: faders, three-band EQ, channel cue, a crossfader, an analogue filter and an external effects send/return loop. Its **2+2** designation can be misleading if you expect four independent channel strips. You get two main stereo channel strips with extra input-level flexibility, not four identical EQ-and-fader strips.

### Why consider it?

- A straightforward layout for mixing between two players or turntables.
- The character of the Xone filter, including adjustable resonance.
- External effects routing if you decide to add a separate FX unit.
- A focused way to practise gain, EQ, transitions and cue monitoring with external decks.

### What are the trade-offs?

**The standard Xone:23 does not have a built-in USB soundcard.** To route separate software decks from a laptop, plan for a suitable multi-output audio interface and supported DJ software. The mixer itself doesn't browse your library, load tracks or offer jog wheels.

**Best for:** someone building a small modular setup around independent players or turntables. **Less suitable:** a beginner with only a laptop who expects the mixer to function as a complete DJ controller.

## 2. Allen & Heath Xone:96: flexible analogue mixing and dual USB audio

<ProductCard productId="allen-heath-xone96" />

The **Xone:96** is not simply a Xone:23 with extra channels. It supports more complex performance setups with multiple inputs, two analogue filter sections, external effects routing and **two independent USB audio interfaces**. Allen & Heath describes its architecture as **6+2**; that count includes different channel and return types, rather than eight identical mixer strips.

### Why consider it?

- Four main stereo channels plus additional signal paths for an expanded performance setup.
- Analogue EQ and dual filters for hands-on, expressive mixing.
- Two USB interfaces for suitable dual-computer changeover or other advanced configurations.
- Routing options for effect processors, extra instruments and hybrid sets.

### What are the trade-offs?

More flexibility means **more cabling, more routing decisions and a higher total system cost**. Dual USB does not mean that any DJ application or DVS configuration will automatically support every available feature. Hardware compatibility, soundcard routing and software licences still matter. You also need actual playback sources.

**Best for:** DJs who already work with several external sources or who need flexible club and hybrid-performance connectivity. If you only blend two tracks at home, much of its capability may go unused.

## 3. Pioneer DJ DJM-A9: professional club mixing with built-in FX

<ProductCard productId="pioneer-djm-a9" />

The **DJM-A9** is a professional four-channel mixer intended to sit at the centre of a club-style setup with connected media players or other sound sources. One of its key differences from the analogue Xone models is the emphasis on **built-in performance effects**. It also provides USB connections for compatible computer changeovers and a Bluetooth audio input that can be routed to a channel.

### Why consider it?

- Four full mixer channels for a typical multi-player club setup.
- **Beat FX** and **Sound Color FX** built into the mixer.
- USB connectivity useful for suitable two-computer DJ changeovers.
- Master, booth and microphone facilities suited to more demanding installations.

### What are the trade-offs?

**The DJM-A9 is still a mixer, not a media player.** It does not load and play your music library by itself. Bluetooth audio input does not turn it into a software controller; Bluetooth latency also makes it a poor assumption for precise beatmatching.

**Best for:** people working with club players and a performance setup that benefits from integrated effects, four channels and professional monitoring options.

## 4. Which mixer should you choose?

### You're starting from zero with a laptop

A **controller that officially supports your DJ software** will usually be simpler and better value. Most beginner controllers combine playback controls, mixing and a usable audio-output path. Start with our [beginner DJ controller guide](/blog/best-dj-controllers-for-beginners/) and [rekordbox vs Serato comparison](/blog/rekordbox-vs-serato/).

### You own two turntables or independent media players

Consider the **Xone:23** if two main channels are enough and you like direct analogue mixing. Before buying, check **phono versus line** connections, grounding and any cables your sources require.

### You need multiple sources, external FX or a dual-laptop configuration

The **Xone:96** deserves a closer look. Draw your actual routing plan: simultaneous decks, effect sends, returns, cue paths, booth outputs and which application will drive which USB interface.

### You want a club-style four-channel setup with mixer effects

The **DJM-A9** makes more sense when the players, installation and performance requirements justify its extra features. A four-channel professional mixer can be superb, but its size and price do not automatically make it a better learning tool.

## 5. Connections to check before spending money

**LINE and PHONO inputs.** A turntable cartridge signal is not the same as line-level audio. Confirm which inputs support each source, including any grounding requirements. Not all channels on every mixer offer the same inputs.

**USB and audio interfaces.** A USB socket doesn't necessarily promise four software decks, DVS support or a free DJ software licence. Check the exact mixer model, operating-system drivers, software edition and routing options.

**MASTER and BOOTH.** At home, a suitable master output may be sufficient. In a club, independently controlled booth speakers can be vital. Check connector type, balanced/unbalanced wiring and the rest of your sound system.

**Headphones and cue.** Look at per-channel pre-listening, cue/master blending and headphone level. Good monitoring matters more than an impressive-looking effects section.

**Effects.** An external send/return loop is different from built-in Beat FX. Account for the extra hardware, cabling and routing if you plan to use external processing.

## 7. Eight common mixer-buying mistakes

1. **Confusing a mixer with a controller:** discovering there's no way to load and play songs from the mixer itself.
2. **Treating “2+2” as four identical channels:** count the actual strips, faders and EQ controls.
3. **Ignoring the cost of players:** the mixer alone isn't a complete setup.
4. **Mixing up phono and line levels:** a mismatch can cause very poor sound or incorrect gain.
5. **Assuming automatic DVS support:** it depends on the exact mixer, interface, software, licences and accessories.
6. **Buying for effects you won't use:** master the fundamentals before paying for extra processing.
7. **Underestimating space and cabling:** especially with large club mixers.
8. **Ordering the wrong variant:** Xone:23 and Xone:23C are not interchangeable.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a DJ mixer and a controller?

A mixer combines audio from connected sources. A controller primarily controls DJ software and may integrate an audio interface and mixer section. Hybrid products exist, so check the precise specification.

### Does a DJ mixer play music by itself?

A conventional mixer **does not browse and play music files independently**. It needs players, turntables, a laptop with appropriate audio outputs or other compatible sources.

### Do you need a computer with a standalone DJ mixer?

Not if you use independent players or turntables. A computer is an option when your interface and software can route audio to the mixer.

### Can I learn DJing on a Xone:23?

Yes, provided you have two suitable audio sources. With only a laptop, a beginner DJ controller is usually simpler.

### Are Xone:23 and Xone:23C the same?

No. They have important connectivity differences, particularly regarding USB/audio-interface functionality. Verify the exact variant.

### What does 2+2 mean on the Xone:23?

There are two main stereo mixing strips with additional input-level options, not four fully independent channel strips with identical faders and EQ.

### What does 6+2 mean on the Xone:96?

It is the maker's description of its main channels and additional signal paths/returns. They do not all have identical EQ, inputs or controls.

### Is Xone:96 better than DJM-A9?

Neither is a universal winner. Xone:96 favours analogue filtering and extensive routing; DJM-A9 appeals to DJs who want integrated effects and a club-style four-channel mixer.

### Does the DJM-A9 have built-in media players?

No. It is a four-channel DJ mixer that works with separately connected sound sources.

### Does the standard Xone:23 have a USB soundcard?

**No.** Do not confuse the Xone:23 with the Xone:23C.

### Can the Xone:96 connect two laptops?

Its dual USB audio interfaces support suitable two-computer setups. You still need to confirm the software, drivers and routing for your actual system.

### Can I connect vinyl turntables?

Yes, to compatible phono inputs, with any required grounding. Turntable cartridge signals need the correct phono stage rather than an ordinary line input.

### Do I need four mixer channels to be a good DJ?

No. Two channels are enough for many sets. Extra channels only become valuable when you actually mix more sources.

### Does a mixer with USB unlock Serato or rekordbox?

Not necessarily. Compatibility and hardware unlock depend on the specific device, software version and licensing terms.

### What's the difference between a filter and an effect?

A filter shapes the frequency content of a signal. Effects such as delay and reverb alter it in other ways. Some mixers integrate both; others depend on external processors.

### Can I plug home speakers straight into a DJ mixer?

It depends on the speaker inputs and whether they are powered. Passive speakers need an amplifier; also verify cables and appropriate signal levels.

### What is a booth output?

An output for DJ monitor speakers. When it has its own level control, you can adjust booth monitoring independently of the main room output.

### Do I need an expensive mixer to produce a professional set?

No. Track selection, phrase matching and confident transitions matter more than the price of the gear. Buy for the workflow you actually need.

## Verdict: buy the right architecture

**Xone:23 for direct two-source mixing; Xone:96 for expanded analogue routing; DJM-A9 for a professional four-channel club workflow with integrated effects.** Before ordering, draw the full setup and add up the costs of players, cables, monitoring and software.

An advanced mixer can be excellent equipment and still be the wrong first purchase. The right choice is the one that fits the rest of your rig.
