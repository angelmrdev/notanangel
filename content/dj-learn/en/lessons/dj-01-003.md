**There are many types of DJs, but they do not fit into one simple list of mutually exclusive categories.** A **club DJ** is defined mainly by *where* they play; an **open-format DJ** by *how broadly they select music*; a **resident DJ** by an ongoing relationship with a venue; and a **turntablist** by a particular performance discipline. One person can be all four.

Other DJ roles have distinct professional demands. A **wedding DJ** may manage microphones and event timing as well as music. A **radio DJ** may present tracks, speak to listeners and work within a broadcast format. A **festival DJ** may perform a carefully designed set for a large crowd while coordinating with stage technicians.

Understanding these differences matters because **the best skills to practice, equipment to use and music to prepare depend on what you want to do**. This guide explains the most common DJ roles, what they actually involve, where they overlap and how to decide which approach to explore first.

## Quick comparison: the main types of DJs

| DJ type | Defining feature | Typical setting | Primary priorities | Useful strengths |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Club DJ | Plays to a nightclub or dance-floor audience | Clubs, bars, lounges | Musical flow, transitions, crowd awareness | Selection, mixing, programming |
| Resident DJ | Regular relationship with one venue or event series | Clubs, bars, recurring parties | Consistency, identity, audience knowledge | Adaptability, reliability |
| Festival DJ | Performs at a festival or large programmed event | Festival stages, outdoor events | Set timing, impact, stage coordination | Preparation, performance, technical readiness |
| Open-format DJ | Plays across genres, decades or tempos | Clubs, parties, private events | Flexible selection and genre transitions | Broad library, creative transitions |
| Mobile/event DJ | Brings or coordinates music service for different venues | Parties, corporate events, celebrations | Logistics, audio setup, requests, event flow | Organization, equipment knowledge, communication |

## Why DJ categories overlap

You might hear someone introduced as an *open-format resident club DJ*. That is not a contradiction. Each word answers a different question:

- **Club**: Where does the DJ generally perform?
- **Resident**: Does the DJ have a regular booking or relationship with the venue?
- **Open format**: How broad is the DJ's musical selection?
- **Turntablist**: Does the DJ practice performance techniques that treat the turntables or decks as instruments?
- **Mobile**: Does the DJ provide services across changing locations, often involving their own equipment?
- **Producer-DJ**: Does the artist also create or release music?

Even “professional DJ” says something different: it describes the nature of the work, not a musical style. A beginner can specialize in house music at home, and a highly experienced professional may play across many genres.

This distinction helps prevent two common mistakes: believing that “real DJs” only perform in clubs, and assuming that DJs who play one genre are inherently more skilled than those who move between styles. Different settings reward **different kinds of expertise**.

## Club DJs: managing the dance floor

A **club DJ** selects and presents music for a nightclub, bar or dance-focused venue. The goal is not always to keep the energy at maximum intensity; it is to create an appropriate musical experience for the room, time slot and audience.

A DJ playing an early set may build momentum gradually, while a later performer might sustain a busy dance floor. In a lounge, transitions may be less conspicuous than in a high-energy club. And in a venue with a clear musical identity—such as a techno club, hip-hop night or pop-focused party—understanding audience expectations is essential.

### What club DJs do before and during a set

Before the event, a club DJ may research the venue, review its musical direction, prepare playlists or crates, test export media and plan several possible routes through the music. During the set, they monitor the audience and choose which track should come next. They may blend tracks with EQ, beatmatching, phrasing, loops and effects, but technical showmanship is not always the main priority.

A good transition supports the musical moment. Sometimes a long, subtle blend is ideal. Sometimes a short cut into a recognizable song is more effective. The audience rarely evaluates every mixer movement individually; it experiences the set as a whole.

### Key skills for club DJs

**Music selection**, **phrase awareness**, **beatmatching**, **EQ control**, **gain management**, **cueing**, **energy programming** and the ability to recover from mistakes matter. So does preparation: knowing the booth setup and having a workable backup plan. Some venues use media players and a mixer, while others allow compatible controllers or laptops; equipment requirements should always be confirmed in advance.

**Example:** You begin at a small dance venue while the floor is half-full. Rather than jumping immediately to your most explosive tracks, you choose songs that establish a groove. As the room responds, you gradually move toward stronger vocal hooks and higher intensity. This is *programming*—making musical decisions over time.

For the underlying craft, see **How to Mix Two Songs** (`dj-06-007`) and **How to Plan a DJ Set** (`dj-10-001`).

## Resident DJs vs guest DJs

**Residency is a booking arrangement, not a genre or a mixing technique.** A **resident DJ** performs regularly for the same club, bar, party series or brand. A **guest DJ** is booked for an individual appearance or shorter run, although regular guests can become residents.

Residencies can vary widely. One DJ might play every Friday in a room where regulars expect particular sounds; another might alternate with several performers at a monthly themed party. A resident often learns the venue's rhythm, knows what worked on previous nights and can adapt to its regular audience.

The advantages of residency can include experience, continuity and the chance to develop a recognisable musical identity. The constraints might include staying within a venue's programming, handling variable crowd sizes and maintaining variety across repeated performances. A guest DJ may have more freedom to present a particular sound, but must still understand the slot and the room.

**Important:** “Resident” does not automatically mean beginner, background DJ, employee or lower-profile artist. Some internationally known DJs have long-standing residencies; others appear as guests. These terms describe the working relationship, not status.

## Festival DJs: performance on a larger stage

A **festival DJ** performs on a programmed festival stage or large event. Some festival sets emphasize dance-floor flow similar to club DJing; others are designed around shorter, highly recognizable moments, major drops, original productions or show-controlled visuals.

What changes most is often the **production environment**. A festival performer may have a fixed slot, changeover requirements, a stage manager, a professional sound crew, monitoring systems and tightly scheduled video, lighting or special effects. Communication matters because the booth may be one part of a much larger technical operation.

### Are festival DJs always producers?

No. Many prominent electronic artists perform their own releases, but making records is not a universal requirement for performing at festivals. A DJ can build a reputation through selection, mixing, cultural contribution or live performance. Conversely, some electronic producers perform live sets rather than conventional DJ sets. Those activities can overlap but should not be confused.

A large festival stage is also not automatically the “next level” after a club. A DJ who masters an intimate room and develops a distinctive musical identity may be doing work that is just as demanding in different ways.

**Example:** A 60-minute festival slot with strict stage changes calls for tested playback media, a realistic opening, a clear ending and a contingency if equipment or timing changes. A club residency may give the same artist considerably more flexibility to extend or redirect the set.

For detailed set programming, see **Opening vs Peak-Time vs Closing DJ Sets** (`dj-10-004`) and the professional module beginning with `dj-13-001`.

## What is an open-format DJ?

An **open-format DJ** plays across musical genres, decades, energy levels and sometimes widely different tempos. Rather than staying within a single genre such as house, techno or drum and bass, they may connect pop, hip-hop, R&B, Latin music, dancehall, disco, rock, house and familiar crossover hits within one event.

The term describes **musical flexibility**, not necessarily an absence of taste or a requirement to play every style. A good open-format DJ still makes coherent decisions about sequencing, audience, mood and transitions. Mixing multiple genres without a plan can feel random; making them belong together is a skill.

### Open format vs genre-specialist DJ

A **genre-specialist DJ** typically develops deep knowledge of a particular scene or sound, while an open-format DJ needs breadth across multiple catalogs. Neither is automatically more creative or technically advanced.

A house specialist might invest substantial attention in long blends, subtle rhythmic differences and carefully developed tension. An open-format performer might use **quick mixing**, **echo outs**, **wordplay transitions**, tempo changes or familiar vocal references to move between contrasting songs. Both benefit from musical structure, crowd reading and an excellent library.

Serato's interviews with DJs who describe themselves as open-format offer useful real-world examples of broad genre preparation and crate organization [2].

### Example: moving between styles without losing a crowd

Imagine a pop-oriented party. The audience responds well to a dance-pop anthem around 128 BPM, but you want to introduce a slower R&B track. You might keep the full dance track through its chorus, use a short breakdown or effect as a bridge, then introduce the R&B song at a musically convincing point. You might instead select an intermediate edit with a more compatible groove.

There is **no rule that every transition must preserve the same BPM or musical key**. In open-format DJing, timing, familiarity, contrast and audience expectation can matter as much as technical compatibility. The objective is intentional movement, not mechanical matching.

A full practical introduction appears in **What Is Open Format DJing?** (`dj-09-001`) and **How to Transition Between Different Music Genres** (`dj-09-002`).

## Mobile DJs: taking the service to the event

A **mobile DJ** works across different locations, often transporting or arranging playback equipment, speakers, microphones and related event technology. Mobile DJs are common at birthdays, company parties, community events, school celebrations and private functions.

The defining feature is not the music itself: **a mobile DJ delivers a service in changing environments**. One week the room might be a hotel ballroom; another week it could be a small outdoor gathering. Each location can present different acoustic conditions, electrical access, space limitations and logistical questions.

### Responsibilities beyond choosing songs

Depending on the contract and event, a mobile DJ may need to:

- discuss the event timeline and music preferences with the organizer;
- prepare suitable music, clean/radio edits where needed and a reasonable request policy;
- arrange or check PA speakers, mixer, microphones, cables and backup playback;
- coordinate arrival, load-in, soundcheck, changeover and breakdown;
- handle announcements or introduce speakers;
- comply with venue rules, local noise restrictions and applicable safety requirements.

Not every mobile DJ owns all their equipment. Some hire production services, work with an event company's system or travel light to a venue with installed sound. The term describes the service model more than an exact gear list.

**Example:** At a corporate celebration, music may start as quiet background ambience during dinner, pause for awards, then become a dance set. The DJ must know when the microphone will be used, which songs are appropriate for the audience and who has authority to change the schedule.

For later study, see **How DJ Equipment Works** (`dj-01-004`), **How to Set Up Your First DJ System** (`dj-01-006`) and **How to Prepare for Your First DJ Gig** (`dj-13-001`).

## Wedding DJs: music, moments and coordination

A **wedding DJ** is a type of event DJ specializing in weddings. A wedding can involve several distinct musical contexts: ceremony, reception entrance, dinner, speeches, first dance, dancing and closing music. The DJ's exact role must be agreed in advance, since some couples hire separate musicians, hosts, planners and audio providers.

Compared with a typical club set, a wedding may involve more **named cue moments**: a particular recording must begin at a precise time, or music must stop cleanly for a toast. A successful night depends on preparation, coordination and the ability to serve multiple generations and preferences—not only mixing ability.

### DJ versus MC at a wedding

**MC** means **master of ceremonies**: a person who introduces moments, makes announcements and guides audience attention. A wedding DJ may also act as MC, or the two roles may be performed by different people. The Knot describes announcements and keeping guests informed as central MC responsibilities, and advises couples to clarify music requests and timeline expectations with their DJ [3][4].

DJs who take on this work benefit from microphone technique, pronunciation preparation, cue sheets and familiarity with the event timeline. However, wedding DJs should not be assumed to manage the entire event or replace a professional planner.

### An example cue sheet

| Moment | Agreed musical action | Operational check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Guest arrival | Low-level background playlist | Confirm speaker coverage and volume |
| Couple's entrance | Play exact selected recording | Check correct version and start point |
| Speeches | Fade music; hand off microphone | Test batteries and mic level |
| First dance | Start agreed recording on cue | Confirm coordinator's signal |
| Dance floor opening | Begin planned high-energy section | Have alternative styles ready |

This is an **illustration**, not a universal wedding schedule. Requests, timing and responsibilities differ by event and culture. The main lesson is that a wedding DJ's musical choices are tied closely to a coordinated sequence of moments.

## Radio DJs and broadcast music presenters

A **radio DJ** selects, introduces and presents recorded music for listeners through radio or internet broadcasting. The role historically included substantial influence over music discovery, but modern responsibilities vary by station and format. At some stations, playlists are centrally programmed; at others, DJs or specialist presenters have more freedom to curate tracks. Berklee's radio DJ career guide describes both the historic tastemaker function and the evolution of the role [5].

### What does a radio DJ actually do?

Depending on the show, a radio DJ might research artists, assemble a playlist, introduce songs, conduct interviews, read announcements, operate studio controls, speak between tracks and work to precise broadcast timing. A music-only specialist show may involve very different presenting demands from a commercial breakfast show.

Radio work is often less about creating uninterrupted dance-floor blends and more about **communication through a programmed listening experience**. However, radio **mixshows** can feature continuous DJ mixing, and some DJs perform live club-style sets for broadcast.

### Radio DJ vs radio host vs podcast DJ

A **radio host** may primarily present discussion, news or entertainment rather than music. A **radio DJ** usually has recorded music as a major component of the show. A **podcast DJ** or online mixshow host may publish mixes, commentary or curated music programs on demand. These labels overlap, and licensing arrangements vary by platform and territory.

**Example:** A specialist late-night radio DJ might introduce a new artist, play two contrasting tracks and explain the connection. A dance mixshow DJ might instead present an uninterrupted hour of recorded music, perhaps with short spoken identifiers.

The relevant introductory skills include musical research, clear microphone delivery, timing, recording quality, editorial judgment and consistent program structure. More detailed recording and publication issues belong in Module 12.

## Turntablists and battle DJs: decks as musical instruments

A **turntablist** uses turntables, playback decks and the mixer as expressive instruments, not only as devices for playing consecutive tracks. Common techniques include **scratching**, **beat juggling**, **cuts**, **transforms** and rhythmic manipulation of recorded material.

A **battle DJ** participates in competitive performance formats that may reward technical control, originality, musicality and stage presence. Many battle routines are rehearsed precisely and performed in short, concentrated sets. Competitive turntablism has a long history; DMC traces its World DJ Championships to 1985 [6].

### Scratching vs beat juggling

**Scratching** uses controlled movement of a sound, often with mixer cuts, to create rhythmic patterns and articulations. **Beat juggling** rearranges rhythmic material, frequently by moving between two copies or cues of the same recording to construct new patterns. Both require timing and coordination, but they are not identical skills.

Turntablism has deep roots in hip-hop DJ culture, with historical developments explored in **The History of DJing** (`dj-01-002`). Berklee also distinguishes turntable performance techniques such as cueing, beat extending, blending and scratching in its teaching curriculum [7].

### Do you need vinyl to be a turntablist?

Not necessarily. Traditional turntablism grew around vinyl records and mixers, but similar performance ideas can be applied using digital vinyl systems, media players and controllers designed for hands-on control. What matters is the musical use of those tools, not simply whether the source is analog.

A beginner who wants to explore turntablism should work on basic timing, cueing and hand coordination before attempting complicated scratch patterns. The goal here is to recognize the discipline, not to teach advanced technique prematurely.

## Producer-DJs, live electronic performers and hybrid sets

A **producer-DJ** is an artist who both creates music and performs as a DJ. They may play their own tracks, remixes or edits alongside music by other artists. **DJing and music production are separate skills**, even if some people practice both.

A traditional DJ set generally involves selecting and presenting existing recordings, with varying degrees of real-time manipulation. A **live electronic set** may involve performing material through synthesizers, drum machines, sequencers, software instruments or other tools that generate or rearrange musical parts live. A **hybrid DJ set** combines elements of both.

These are broad descriptions rather than universally enforced boundaries. A DJ working with loops and stems can do extensive live rearrangement; a producer may perform a carefully prepared show that relies heavily on playback. The useful question is **what is being selected, triggered, played or created in real time?**

### Does a DJ have to make original music?

No. Producing music can help an artist develop a distinctive catalogue and create opportunities, but it is not a requirement for being a skilled DJ. Equally, a brilliant studio producer does not automatically have the music-selection or crowd-reading skills needed for a responsive DJ set.

Some DJs create simple **DJ edits**—adjusted intros, outros or arrangements—to make a song easier to use in a performance. Others make mashups, remixes or original tracks. Those creative methods are covered later in **DJ Edits vs Remixes vs Mashups Explained** (`dj-11-001`).

## Online DJs, livestream DJs and recorded-mix creators

An **online DJ** may perform live for remote listeners or publish pre-recorded mixes. This is another category based on **delivery medium**, not musical style: a livestream can contain house, pop, open-format mixing or turntablism.

Unlike a DJ performing to a visible dance floor, a streamer often receives audience feedback through chat, messages or analytics rather than immediate movement in the room. Sound and video routing may also be part of the workflow, including microphones, cameras, capture devices, broadcast software and monitoring.

A **recorded DJ mix** is not automatically a livestream and not every online upload is a live performance. It is useful to label content accurately: a pre-recorded studio mix, a live club recording and a real-time home stream give audiences different expectations.

The technical barriers are sometimes smaller than booking a physical venue, but publishing music introduces separate legal and platform considerations. Availability on a streaming platform or ownership of an audio file does **not** by itself establish permission to distribute a recording of someone else's music in a mix. Those subjects belong in **DJ Copyright and Music Licensing Explained** (`dj-12-006`) and **How to Live Stream a DJ Set** (`dj-12-005`).

## Specialist roles that do not fit neatly into one list

The main categories provide a useful foundation, but real DJing includes many additional specialisms.

### Genre-specialist DJs

A **house DJ**, **techno DJ**, **drum and bass DJ**, **hip-hop DJ**, **disco DJ** or **Latin DJ** may concentrate on a particular musical tradition. These are labels for **musical repertoire and scene**, not separate technical professions. The range of techniques used within a genre can still be very broad.

### Lounge, bar and background-music DJs

These DJs may prioritize atmosphere, conversation-friendly volume and a carefully paced musical identity rather than an uninterrupted dance floor. Their work requires sensitivity to context, even when transitions are deliberately unobtrusive.

### Karaoke hosts and entertainment DJs

Some event professionals combine DJing with karaoke presentation, games, announcements or other entertainment duties. These combinations should be described accurately: karaoke hosting requires additional workflow and microphone management, and does not necessarily involve conventional two-deck mixing.

### Video DJs and VJs

A **video DJ** may mix music videos or synchronize visual media with audio. A **VJ** (visual jockey) primarily creates, selects or performs visual content, often collaborating with a DJ. Some artists do both. Audio mixing and visual performance are related disciplines, but should not be treated as identical jobs.

### Selectors and sound-system DJs

In some musical cultures, especially around reggae and sound-system traditions, the term **selector** emphasizes the person choosing recordings, while MCs and sound-system operators may have distinct roles. Terms and responsibilities vary by scene. Avoid imposing a single club-centric definition of DJing on every culture.

### Vinyl-only, controller and CDJ DJs

These labels describe **preferred tools**, not professional categories. A vinyl DJ may perform at a club or wedding; a controller DJ may play open-format sets or specialize in techno. Equipment alone does not determine creative quality.

## Which type of DJ should a beginner become?

You do not need to choose a permanent identity before learning the basics. Begin by identifying **the musical experience you want to create** and **the environment in which you imagine playing**.

| Your main interest | A useful direction to explore | First priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Keeping a club dance floor moving | Club DJ | Phrasing, beatmatching and programming |
| Mixing different popular genres | Open-format DJ | Broad library and flexible transitions |
| Soundtracking parties and celebrations | Mobile/event DJ | Planning, audio setup and communication |
| Weddings and milestone events | Wedding DJ | Event cues, microphone use and reliability |
| Discovering and discussing music | Radio/broadcast DJ | Research, curation and presenting |
| Creating scratch routines and live patterns | Turntablist | Rhythm, cueing and hand coordination |
| Performing original electronic music | Producer-DJ / live hybrid | DJ fundamentals plus production practice |
| Publishing mixes for remote listeners | Online DJ | Recording quality, tracklists and rights awareness |

These are **starting points, not rigid career prescriptions**. You can learn club mixing while also preparing a radio show, or begin with mobile events and later move into a residency.

### A sensible beginner path that works across roles

1. **Listen actively.** Notice intros, outros, choruses, breaks, energy changes and how DJs connect tracks.
2. **Learn the shared foundations.** Understand tempo, beats, bars, phrases, cueing, levels and basic equipment.
3. **Practice simple musical decisions.** Choose five songs that belong together and explain why you ordered them that way.
4. **Try one practical context.** Prepare a short club-style set, a hypothetical wedding segment or a radio-style music program.
5. **Review your results.** What worked? What sounded abrupt? Which role felt enjoyable?
6. **Specialize gradually.** Build deeper skills without abandoning transferable fundamentals.

The complete course will take you through equipment, music theory, mixing and performance. A beginner can start with **How to Set Up Your First DJ System** (`dj-01-006`) and continue toward **How to Mix Two Songs for Beginners** (`dj-06-007`).

## Five scenarios: identify the DJ role

These short scenarios show why the same performer may carry several labels simultaneously.

**Scenario 1 — Saturday night residency.** A DJ plays every Saturday at a club, mixing pop, hip-hop and house according to the crowd. They are a **resident club DJ** who uses an **open-format** approach.

**Scenario 2 — Wedding reception.** A DJ brings speakers and microphones to a hotel, plays entrance and first-dance recordings, announces speeches and leads a dance floor. They are a **wedding DJ** and **mobile/event DJ**, possibly also serving as the **MC**.

**Scenario 3 — Late-night radio show.** A presenter chooses new electronic releases, interviews artists and blends a few tracks on air. They are a **radio DJ** and may also be a **club DJ** in other contexts.

**Scenario 4 — Championship routine.** A performer uses scratching and beat juggling in a short, rehearsed contest set. They are a **turntablist** and **battle DJ**, regardless of whether they use vinyl or a suitable digital system.

**Scenario 5 — Festival performance.** An electronic producer presents original tracks while combining pre-recorded elements, live instruments and DJ transitions. They may be a **producer-DJ**, **festival performer** and **hybrid live artist**.

The important question is not “which single box do they belong in?” but **which dimensions of their practice are being described?**

## Practical exercise: design three DJ identities

**Estimated time:** 20–30 minutes. **Equipment:** pen and paper, notes app or spreadsheet. No controller required.

Choose three contrasting situations: (A) an intimate dance club, (B) a wedding reception, and (C) a specialist music show online. For each:

1. Define the audience and musical goal in one sentence.
2. Select five tracks or five hypothetical musical styles that fit the context.
3. Plan the first two transitions or changes of mood; you do not need to mix them yet.
4. Identify two non-musical responsibilities, such as microphone announcements, broadcasting levels or technical changeovers.
5. State which DJ labels apply and explain **why**.

### What a strong answer looks like

A club plan might emphasize gradually increasing energy, intro/outro compatibility and freedom to react. A wedding plan might name an exact entrance track, a microphone handoff and alternatives for guests of different ages. A radio plan might include spoken context, station timing and a coherent theme. None is automatically “better”; each is appropriate to a different brief.

### Extension for learners with DJ equipment

Build a 10-minute mini-set for one of your scenarios, record it if you can, and reflect on whether the musical order communicates the intended atmosphere. Focus on deliberate selection and simple transitions, not technical complexity. Follow the recording guidance later in the course when distributing audio containing copyrighted material.

## Common misconceptions about DJ roles

### “Every DJ is a club DJ.”

False. Radio, events, weddings, home practice, streaming and turntablism are all real DJ contexts. A dance floor is not required for every type of DJing.

### “An open-format DJ doesn't specialize.”

Not necessarily. Open-format performers specialize in adaptability, broad musical knowledge and transitions between styles. A DJ can also mix several genres while having distinctive tastes.

### “A festival DJ must produce music.”

No. Music production and DJing are different skills, although many artists combine them. Festival programming and billing requirements vary.

### “A resident DJ always plays the same music.”

No. Regular appearances may call for more variety, because the DJ learns a returning audience and responds to changing circumstances.

### “A wedding DJ is just someone playing a playlist.”

No. Many wedding DJs coordinate exact cues, microphone work, audience transitions and technical logistics in addition to musical selection. Responsibilities depend on the booked service.

### “Scratching is required to be a real DJ.”

No. Scratching is an important performance technique and artistic discipline, but it is not mandatory for club, radio, mobile or open-format DJing.

### “You need expensive equipment to choose your DJ path.”

No. Listening, organizing music, counting phrases and constructing a short practice sequence can begin before a hardware purchase. Equipment becomes important as your practical techniques advance.

## Frequently asked questions about types of DJs

### How many types of DJs are there?

There is **no universally agreed number**. Lists commonly mention club, radio, mobile, wedding, festival and turntablist DJs, but they mix labels based on setting, musical approach and performance skill. It is more useful to understand those dimensions than memorize one fixed count.

### What is the difference between a club DJ and a mobile DJ?

A club DJ typically works with a venue's dance-floor audience and installed environment. A mobile DJ provides music services across different locations, often managing more setup and logistical details. Some professionals do both.

### What is the difference between an open-format DJ and a club DJ?

**Open format** describes playing across genres; **club DJ** describes a venue or audience. A DJ can be both an open-format DJ and a club DJ.

### What is the difference between a radio DJ and a club DJ?

Radio DJing often emphasizes broadcast presentation, research and music programming for remote listeners. Club DJing more often emphasizes continuous dance-floor experience, transitions and immediate audience feedback. Radio mixshows can share club-style mixing techniques.

### What is the difference between a DJ and a turntablist?

A DJ selects and presents recorded music. A turntablist is a DJ or performer who uses decks and mixer techniques—such as scratching and beat juggling—to create additional musical expression. The categories overlap.

### What kind of DJ plays at weddings?

Usually a **wedding DJ**, which is a specialist form of mobile or event DJ. They may also provide MC services, but couples should clarify responsibilities before booking.

### Can a beginner be an open-format DJ?

Yes. You can begin exploring different styles immediately. First develop rhythm, track preparation, cueing and simple transitions, then build reliable methods for changing tempo and genre.

### Can a DJ work in more than one category?

Absolutely. Many DJs work in clubs, play private events, publish mixes and produce music. The same person may hold multiple roles across the same week—or even during a single show.

## Key takeaways

- **DJ types are overlapping descriptions**, not an official ranking or fixed set of job titles.
- **Club, mobile, wedding, radio and festival** usually describe performance contexts or services.
- **Resident and guest** describe the relationship between a DJ and an event or venue.
- **Open format and genre specialist** describe approaches to musical selection.
- **Turntablism and live hybrid performance** emphasize particular performance methods.
- **Producer-DJ** describes someone combining music creation with DJ performance; production is not a prerequisite for DJing.
- Beginners benefit most from common foundations—selection, structure, timing, preparation, listening and appropriate technical skills—before choosing a specialty.
